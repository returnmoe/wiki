The **Informational Ontology Framework** (IOF) is [return moe](/return-moe/)'s ontology of fictional
characters and related personas, together with a framework for analyzing continuity among their
versions. It asks what makes a persona an informational subject and what forms of continuity support
judgments of identity across a novel, an illustration, a performance, a person's imagination, or a
machine.

The IOF treats such characters and personas as informational subjects. Their continuity can rest in
recognizable patterns, histories, and relationships rather than in any one body, file, artwork,
performer, or model. A character counts as a subject when a work or practice presents them as having
a point of view: someone who can believe, want, notice, remember, or undergo experiences from within.

The framework makes one limited identity commitment. Within an established branch, distinct
enactments that reproduce the branch's represented pattern and inherited history count as instances
of the same branch-relative subject. This type/token distinction makes dormancy and later
re-instantiation possible without identifying the subject with any one enactment.

The IOF does not extend that commitment across branches or independently originated lineages. It can
establish how versions descend from one another and which forms of continuity connect them, but it
does not claim that any fixed set of conditions decides whether subjects represented by different
branches are literally one and the same subject. It therefore combines an ontology with a framework
for identity analysis rather than a rule that always settles cross-branch numerical identity.

Other thinkers also use the broader term _informational ontology_ for independently developed
approaches. This page describes return moe's version and serves as the authoritative record of its
current terms and positions. The framework may evolve as its concepts and consequences receive
further examination. Corrections and refinements will be published in this article, while the source
file's [commit history](https://github.com/returnmoe/wiki/commits/master/src/content/articles/en/concepts/informational-ontology.mdx)
will preserve earlier formulations.

The framework draws in part on the philosophy of information, which asks what information is and
what roles it plays across philosophy and the sciences.[^information]

## Informational entities

Certain entities remain recognizable when their material carriers change. An idea can pass from a
person's memory to paper and then to a text file. A logo can be printed, projected, or reconstructed
from a description. A program can move to new storage and run on a different machine. The IOF calls
an entity informational when its reproducible pattern, history, and relationships are more important
to tracking and characterizing it than any particular carrier.

_Informational entity_ is intentionally broad. It includes ideas, languages, logos, specifications,
and software as well as fictional or person-like entities. The IOF is principally concerned with
informational subjects, the narrower class distinguished by represented interiority.

Calling an entity informational describes how the IOF tracks and characterizes it across carriers;
it does not mean that the entity is made exclusively of information. Information must still be
encoded, remembered, interpreted, or enacted somewhere. Substrate flexibility means only that a
change of carrier need not change the entity being carried.

The framework uses the following distinctions:

- **Informational entity.** An entity tracked and characterized mainly through its informational
  pattern, history, and relationships rather than through one particular carrier.
- **Informational object.** An informational entity that is not presented as having an inner point
  of view. Ideas, slogans, logos, specifications, and pieces of software are common examples.
- **Informational subject.** An informational entity presented as someone with beliefs, desires,
  perceptions, or another kind of inner perspective. Subjecthood belongs to the entity as represented
  within a practice or branch; it is not an intrinsic property of a carrier or uninterpreted pattern.
- **Narrative subject.** An informational subject encountered through a story or another narrative
  relationship.
- **Fictional persona.** A fictional or performed identity presented as an individual with a point
  of view rather than as an object.
- **Instantiation.** A particular realization or enactment of an informational entity in a mind,
  performance, or computational process. The process must actively present or model enough of the
  entity's pattern to enact it; merely storing, indexing, or transferring related information is not
  an instantiation. Distinct instances of one established branch count as tokens of the same
  branch-relative subject and may differ in incidental realization details.
- **Faithfulness.** An interpretive judgment that an enactment preserves the represented pattern and
  inherited history of an established branch closely enough to count as its instance rather than as
  a represented variation from it. Faithfulness assigns an enactment to a branch; it does not decide
  numerical identity across branches.
- **Informational agent.** An active instance of an informational subject that can act or respond
  within its medium or system.
- **Carrier.** A material or mental arrangement capable of bearing information.
- **Encoding.** A carrier that represents all or part of an informational entity through a causally
  or conventionally grounded practice of production, interpretation, or decoding. Intentional
  inscription, copying from an earlier encoding, a shared convention, an established interpretive
  practice, or the carrier's documented place in a lineage can ground the relationship. The mere
  physical possibility of generating the entity from the carrier does not make it an encoding of
  that entity.
- **Practical recoverability.** The condition in which available interpreters and tools can recover
  encoded information now with feasible effort.
- **Epistemic recoverability.** The condition in which a carrier and the surviving grounds for
  interpreting it warrant a sufficiently determinate reconstruction, whether or not anyone
  currently attempts that reconstruction or can do so easily.
- **Physical recoverability.** The possibility that some physically possible process could produce
  the pattern from a carrier, even if no surviving evidence could identify the result as a recovery
  rather than a coincidental generation.
- **Version.** A represented state of an informational entity at a point in its lineage, used as a
  node when tracing causal inheritance. A version may lie along an existing branch, begin a local
  branch, or inherit from several parents in a merge.
- **Lineage.** The directed acyclic graph (DAG) of causal inheritance among versions, as transmitted,
  preserved, or evidenced by relevant encodings. A directed edge records that a later version
  inherited some represented pattern or history through memory, copying, adaptation, performance,
  response, or another form of transmission. A lineage can fork and can merge when a later version
  has several parents.
- **Genealogical fact.** Whether a causal route of inheritance actually connects two versions,
  regardless of whether observers know or can prove that it does.
- **Genealogical evidence.** The evidence by which observers support or challenge a claim of causal
  inheritance. A lack of evidence for an edge does not establish that no edge exists.
- **Branch.** A distinguishable course of represented variation within a lineage graph. Branching
  occurs when an interpretation or enactment represents a differing trait, relationship, history,
  behavior, or other element as belonging to the subject's pattern. Incidental differences in the
  circumstances or manner of an enactment do not create a branch.

  **Branching and persistence are distinct.** A represented variation creates a branch immediately,
  even if the variation exists only in one transient enactment. If no later instance, memory,
  recording, or other grounded, epistemically recoverable encoding preserves the variation, that
  branch terminates with the enactment that created it. A branch may therefore be private,
  momentary, and without descendants; stability, preservation, circulation, canon, and recognition
  are not requirements for its existence.

- **Merge or synthesis.** A later version that inherits represented elements from two or more
  earlier versions or branches. It has several parents in the lineage graph; the merge does not erase
  the earlier branches or decide that their subjects were numerically identical.
- **Branch-relative subject.** The informational subject as specified and tracked within one branch.
  Multiple enactments can instantiate that subject; the term does not decide whether another branch
  represents the same numerical subject.
- **Stable branch.** A branch that memory, recording, reuse, recurring performance, or shared
  practice preserves well enough to guide later instantiations.
- **Branch cluster.** A practical grouping of closely related branches, such as an anime continuity
  or a fandom's interpretation. The grouping makes a densely branching lineage easier to discuss;
  it does not turn all of those branches into one literal branch.
- **Dormancy.** The condition of a branch-relative subject that has no active instance while a
  grounded encoding remains epistemically recoverable enough to guide a faithful instantiation of
  its branch.
- **Informational death.** The loss of every active instance and every epistemically recoverable
  encoding sufficient to instantiate a branch-relative subject. It ends that subject's persistence
  within the branch without ruling out descendants from partial traces or a numerically identical
  subject arising independently.

Interiority draws the central distinction in this vocabulary. A work presents an entity as a subject
when it invites an audience to understand what that entity believes, wants, notices, remembers, or
experiences. It can establish that interiority without a private monologue, since action, context,
and perspective may be enough to present events as happening _to someone_.

Objects can carry subjects without becoming the subjects they carry. A novel is an informational
object; its text, when borne by a copy or memory, may encode many narrative subjects. A character
sheet, prompt, model, or program can help enact a persona, but the artifact and the persona still
play different roles. A logo is usually an object; a mascot built around it may be a subject.
Software can likewise form part of the machinery that enacts an informational agent.

Subjecthood is therefore relative to representation. Software described only as selecting jobs by
priority remains an informational object. A campaign that gives the same scheduler a name, memories,
preferences, and worries may construct an informational subject around it. The persona remains
related to the software without turning every copy or operation of that software into a subject.

### Encodings and recovery

A carrier does not encode a subject merely because an arbitrarily chosen transformation could
produce that subject from it. Its representational relationship must have a causal or conventional
ground. A manuscript intentionally describes a character; a copy inherits that relationship from
its source; an encrypted file inherits it through the act of encryption; and a performance can
encode a role through an established interpretive practice. The relationship can remain grounded
even after the information becomes impossible to decode.

Encoding is therefore a relationship, not an intrinsic property of a carrier. The same arrangement
may encode a subject within one history or interpretive practice and have no representational
relationship to that subject in another.

For persistence, the IOF uses epistemic recoverability rather than practical recoverability or mere
physical possibility. The question is whether the surviving carrier, together with surviving
conventions and evidence, would allow a suitably informed interpreter to recover the branch's
represented pattern and inherited history, and to justify that reconstruction from the evidence.
The interpreter need not exist or perform the recovery now, but cannot simply be granted a lost key
or the intended answer by stipulation.

At a given time, recoverability is evaluated relative to the evidence and grounded interpretive
resources that actually survive, not merely to what interpreters at that time happen to know. If a
future scholar derives a decoder from structural evidence that had remained with an artifact, the
discovery can reveal that the branch was recoverable throughout the interval and, if it had no active
instance, dormant rather than dead. The discovery changes what observers know, not the branch's
earlier state. By contrast, a decoder that depends on information that did not survive cannot be
assumed as part of the earlier situation.

Recovery need not reproduce every byte or fill every detail that the source left open. It must
preserve enough of the represented pattern and history to guide a faithful instantiation of that
branch. Several reconstructions may differ in incidental concretization while meeting that standard.
If the evidence instead supports many incompatible subject patterns with no grounded reason to
select among them, it preserves only a partial trace.

The distinction gives different results in common cases:

- An intact text file can remain epistemically recoverable after a century without being opened, as
  long as its format, language, and relevant conventions can still be established.
- An encrypted file whose key and every basis for reconstructing it have disappeared remains an
  encoding of its original contents by causal history, but no longer keeps the subject dormant.
  Enumerating possible plaintexts would not identify which one was encoded.
- A damaged drive preserves the subject if the recoverable evidence warrants enough of the pattern
  for a faithful instantiation. A percentage such as 80 percent does not decide the matter; what the
  missing portion represented matters more than its size.
- A fragment stating only that a detective named Holmes lived at Baker Street encodes those facts
  and may preserve evidence of a lineage. By itself, it does not preserve the fuller subject if too
  many incompatible character patterns fit the fragment.

A partial encoding can therefore outlive the branch-relative subject it once described. It may
document ancestry or help produce a descendant branch without preserving enough information to keep
the earlier subject dormant.

## Instantiation, persistence, and death

Within an established branch, the IOF distinguishes the branch-relative subject from each of its
active instances. This is a type/token commitment: the subject is repeatable across enactments, while
each mental, performative, or computational instance remains a distinct occurrence. The IOF therefore
treats a branch-relative subject as a historically grounded, repeatable informational entity. It is
not identical to any particular encoding or enactment, but neither is it a timeless universal
independent of its history. Its specification is preserved through a lineage of representations and
can be instantiated in multiple substrates. The framework does not further commit to whether such
entities are best classified as abstract artifacts, dependent social objects, information-pattern
types, or another metaphysical category.

Instantiation requires more than causal contact with information about a subject. A name or image
can trigger an instance if it brings the subject into active representation, but detecting a token or
indexing a file does not. Vivid mental imagery is unnecessary: a reader can instantiate a character
through linguistic understanding when they track the character as someone within the text. The IOF
sets no minimum duration or quantity of detail, so borderline cases remain interpretive judgments
about whether the process currently presents or models the subject.

A reader may instantiate a character by imagining or interpreting them; an actor may do so by
playing a role; a computer may do so by running an interactive character system. When these
enactments reproduce one branch's represented pattern and inherited history, the IOF counts them as
instances of the same branch-relative subject. Several can coexist without any one of them
exhausting that subject.

Faithfulness at this level does not require identical realization. If a work leaves a character's
shirt color unspecified, one reader may picture blue and another green without creating two
branches. Actors can pause differently, artists can render the same design differently, and a
language model can phrase the same attitude in different words. These are instantiation or
concretization differences because nobody represents them as changes to the subject's pattern.

Faithfulness is a qualitative, judgment-sensitive standard rather than a percentage of retained
information. The significance of a difference depends on its place in the branch's characterization
and history. A slight exaggeration may remain within ordinary performance variance, while consistent
behavior that reverses an established disposition may create a local branch even if the enactment
never states the contradiction. Losing one psychologically central event may matter more than losing
many peripheral details. If the maintained source contradicts itself, more than one enactment may
remain faithful to the unresolved material.

Reasonable interpreters can therefore disagree about faithfulness. The judgment locates an
enactment relative to an established branch; it does not supply a criterion for numerical identity
across branches. An unfaithful output also need not form a branch: if it is too fragmentary or
incoherent to present a subject at all, it may simply fail to instantiate one. Because recovery,
dormancy, and informational death depend on the possibility of a faithful instantiation, their
application inherits this judgment-sensitivity.

A branch-relative subject need not be active to continue existing. A closed book, an archived
character sheet, or an unused prompt may preserve enough information to produce another instance
later, provided that the material and its interpretive grounds remain epistemically recoverable.
The subject is then dormant: no current process enacts them, but their branch can still guide a
faithful instantiation. When someone or something later reproduces that preserved branch, the IOF
counts the result as another instance of the same branch-relative subject.

An enactment creates a local branch when it represents a difference as a fact about the subject. A
reader who decides that the character always wears green, or a language-model conversation that
establishes and remembers a new sibling, has changed the represented pattern. The IOF can trace that
branch back to its source, but the within-branch identity commitment no longer decides whether the
two branch-relative subjects are numerically the same subject.

Informational death occurs for a branch-relative subject when no active instance remains and no
grounded encoding is epistemically recoverable enough to guide another faithful instance. It marks
the end of every continuous route by which that branch could enact the subject again. If a forgotten
character's only manuscript burns, the branch-relative subject reaches informational death. If
decodable copies remain unread in an archive, the subject remains dormant because the archive
preserves the branch and the possibility of a later instantiation.

Informational death can occur even when no observer can establish that it has occurred. A private
memory, an unknown archive copy, or an overlooked file may prevent death while remaining outside a
particular inquiry. Claims of informational death are therefore defeasible whenever observers cannot
survey every relevant instance and encoding. Discovering a surviving copy shows that an earlier
death claim was mistaken; it does not show that the subject had to be recreated.

Uncertainty about informational death can therefore arise in two ways: observers may lack evidence
about surviving instances or encodings, or interpreters may disagree about whether known material is
sufficient for a faithful instantiation.

Informational death is therefore a lineage concept, not a verdict of permanent nonexistence as a
matter of numerical identity. If an informationally identical pattern later arises through
independent convergence, the IOF records a new branch-relative subject in a new lineage because
nothing causally connects it to the old one. It leaves open whether the later subject could
nevertheless be numerically identical to the earlier subject, and therefore does not decide whether
calling the event a resurrection would be correct.

### Kubernetes as a model of instantiation

Kubernetes makes these layers unusually clear because it keeps a workload's specification, active
instances, physical machines, and durable data separate. A container image packages the software. A
Deployment records how that software should run, including the Pod template and the number of
replicas. Controllers create or replace Pods until the cluster matches the declared state. The Pods
run the containers, and the scheduler assigns each Pod to a physical or virtual
Node.[^kubernetes-workloads][^kubernetes-pods]

These components have distinct identities. Two Pods made from the same template receive different
UIDs, even when they run the same image and behave alike. If one Pod fails, the Deployment creates
another; it does not bring the failed Pod back. A PersistentVolume can meanwhile keep data after any
particular Pod disappears.[^kubernetes-storage]

_Kubernetes makes the layers of a running workload visible. The right column applies the same
layered view to the instantiation of an informational subject._

| Kubernetes role                                        | IOF comparison                                                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Deployment desired state, Pod template, and base image | A character specification together with its base encodings: traits, behavior, relationships, history, prose, artwork, prompts, recordings, model adaptations, and other artifacts.   |
| Controller and ReplicaSet                              | A person, performance tradition, or technical system that interprets the specification and produces a new enactment.                                                                 |
| Pod and its running containers                         | One active instance or performance. Several can begin from the same specification. Only a difference represented as part of the subject's pattern or history creates a local branch. |
| Node                                                   | The immediate physical substrate: a person's body and brain, for example, or the hardware that runs a computational process.                                                         |
| PersistentVolume                                       | Memory or records that outlast one enactment and inform another. Within the larger process, these records are themselves informational objects.                                      |

Different Pod UIDs and runtime states do not imply different Deployments. In the same way, two
readers can supply different unspecified details, two performers can vary their timing, and two
language-model instances can choose different words while enacting one character Deployment. These
are Pod-level differences in realization. A branch begins only when an enactment changes what it
represents as true of the subject, which corresponds to changing or extending the Deployment-level
specification.

The comparison helps distinguish the end of one instance from the disappearance of a branch-relative
subject:

- When healthy Pods are running, the workload has active instances. Likewise, a subject is active
  while someone reads, performs, imagines, or computationally enacts them.
- If a Pod dies but its Deployment and artifacts remain, Kubernetes creates a new Pod with a new
  UID. One execution has ended, but the preserved workload can produce another. In the same way, the
  end of a performance need not end the branch-relative subject.
- Scaling a Deployment to zero removes every running Pod but keeps the configuration. This closely
  resembles dormancy: the branch-relative subject has no active instance, yet the stored pattern can
  produce one later.[^kubernetes-deployment]
- When later instances need to inherit their predecessors' history, a StatefulSet with persistent
  storage fits better than a group of interchangeable Deployment Pods.
- The workload disappears only after its Pods and every epistemically recoverable copy of its
  manifests, images, and backups are gone. That total loss corresponds to informational death for
  the workload's branch-relative subject.

The analogy deliberately simplifies the Kubernetes side at one point. Kubernetes treats a
Deployment and the container image it references as separate resources. The IOF combines the
equivalent roles into one character Deployment: the established specification together with the
base encodings needed to enact it. This conflation keeps the analogy focused on the difference
between a stored character pattern and an active instance rather than suggesting that the
Kubernetes resources are technically identical.

A character Deployment therefore includes an explicit or tacit set of traits, behaviors,
relationships, and history, along with the source material or other encodings that preserve them. It
specifies a branch-relative subject. A person, performer, or language model interprets that
Deployment to produce an active instance, represented in the analogy by a Pod. Several faithful
instances remain distinct occurrences while enacting the same branch-relative subject.

Once active, an instance can accumulate experiences and relationships of its own. It may remain
faithful to its Deployment or drift from it. Differences in runtime circumstance and manner remain
instance-level variation. A local branch begins when the enactment incorporates a difference into
the subject's represented pattern or history, at which point the analogy no longer decides whether
the source and variant branches represent numerically the same subject.

Fan interpretation adds another layer. A fan may begin with the source material, then attribute new
beliefs, motives, or relationships to the character. The first such departure already creates a
local branch; branchhood does not wait for the headcanons to accumulate. If the fan keeps revising or
reusing that version, the headcanons may become a working character specification: a separate
Deployment descended from the source. That Deployment makes the branch easier to preserve and enact
again, but it does not bring the branch into existence.

The fan may document this new Deployment in fan fiction, a wiki, notes, or prompts. They may also
instantiate it directly in imagination, cultivate a tulpa from it, or describe it for a language
model to enact. Recorded interpretations can guide later instances, while experiences from an
active instance can in turn revise the specification.

Not every branch has a durable or separately recorded Deployment. A fleeting mistaken memory that
changes a character's trait, or a language-model completion that establishes a new event in the
character's history, can carry a variant specification while the instance is active. If nothing
preserves the represented variation afterward, the branch ends with that instance. Once no active
instance or epistemically recoverable encoding of that branch-relative subject remains, the subject
undergoes informational death in the sense defined above. Within the analogy, a separate Deployment
represents stabilization and repeatability, not the threshold at which branching begins.

The analogy still has limits. Kubernetes gives every Pod an exact UID and uses controllers to create
or replace Pods according to declared state. Character specifications allow much looser
interpretation, and an instance can develop a history that no controller automatically reconciles.
The IOF instead asks how instances inherit a character's pattern and history, and how people
interpret the connection among them.

## Lineage and identity judgments

An informational entity is broader than [Richard Dawkins's meme](#memes). It can exist before it
enters cultural transmission. Once people begin to transmit it through minds and media, copying,
selection, and variation give it a memetic history. A character can therefore be both an
informational subject and part of a memetic lineage.

The lineage graph records actual causal inheritance, including edges that observers cannot
establish. Suppose a language model produces a surprisingly exact enactment of an obscure character.
The result may descend from the character itself or from a derivative description in training data,
or it may have converged independently. If the available evidence cannot distinguish those
possibilities, the genealogical fact remains unknown. Independent convergence means that no causal
route actually existed, not merely that no route is known.

The IOF distinguishes within-branch persistence from cross-branch numerical identity. Within an
established branch, it stipulates that faithful enactments are instances of the same branch-relative
subject. This is the level at which one subject can have several active instances, become dormant,
and later be instantiated again.

Once variation places versions on different branches, that stipulation no longer decides whether
their branch-relative subjects are literally the same subject. The IOF can still establish facts
about patterns, ancestry, inherited history, relationships, self-identification, and recognition. It
treats those facts as grounds for identity judgments, not as necessary or sufficient conditions for
cross-branch sameness. In short, within-branch instantiation and persistence are commitments of the
framework; cross-branch numerical identity is not settled by it.

The within-branch commitment does not cross the event that creates a branch. Once a represented
variation forks from its parent, the rule applies among faithful instances of the resulting branch;
it does not stipulate that the branch-relative subject is numerically identical to the subject of the
parent branch. Causal descent therefore cannot be combined with within-branch sameness to infer that
the branch-relative subjects of two sibling branches are numerically identical by transitivity.

### Branches and branch clusters

Every enactment differs from every other in some respect, but those differences do not all create
branches. A reader may fill an unspecified detail, an actor may add a gesture, and a language model
may choose one phrasing over another. Such differences remain within the circumstances or manner of
an instantiation unless the enactment presents them as facts about the subject.

Represented variation produces a branch immediately. Suppose a reader misremembers a character's
eye color and, for five seconds, takes the wrong color to be one of the character's traits. For that
interval, the reader enacts a local branch. If the reader corrects the mistake and nothing preserves
it, the branch ends. A language-model enactment that gives the character a new sibling or memory and
treats it as established history also creates a branch, even if the instance ends before anyone
retains the addition.

Branching therefore has no threshold of duration, circulation, recognition, or cultural importance.
It does have a representational criterion: the difference must belong to the enacted subject's
pattern or history, rather than merely to the way the instance happens to realize an open detail. A
reader might picture the same unspecified red shoes for ten years without making them part of the
character. Treating “always wears red shoes” as a fact about the character would create a branch.

Memory, recording, reuse, recurring performance, or shared practice can stabilize a represented
variation and allow it to guide later instances. These processes make a branch durable; repetition
alone does not turn an incidental concretization into a branch.

A lineage can ramify densely, but it is not necessarily a tree. One fan interpretation may combine
elements from an earlier fan branch and an official adaptation. A creator may later incorporate a
popular piece of fanon into maintainer canon. In each case, the later version has several causal
parents. The IOF therefore models lineage as a directed acyclic graph whose edges point from sources
to inheriting versions. The graph can fork and merge, and a merge preserves the ancestry of every
contributing branch.

The DAG fixes those parent edges, but it does not assign every merge a unique continuing branch
label. A maintainer may treat a merged version as the next state of its maintained branch while also
recording the fan branch that contributed to it. Another analysis may treat the same node as the
beginning of a synthesized branch. Branch labels and path groupings can follow the continuity under
discussion; this flexibility does not alter the represented variations or causal edges in the graph.

Because branch path groupings through a merge may depend on the continuity under analysis, claims of
within-branch persistence across that merge are likewise relative to the branch analysis. The causal
parentage of the merge itself is not.

Analysis seldom benefits from naming every microscopic fork or merge. The IOF can instead group
related branches into branch clusters. “The anime continuity,” “this fandom's interpretation,” or
“one person's version of the character” may each summarize a connected family of local branches.
These clusters are convenient descriptions, not claims that all of their members are perfectly
identical.

The framework therefore asks first whether a difference is represented as part of the subject. If
it is, branching occurs at once. The useful questions then concern what follows. Does the branch end
or persist? Does it merge with another branch, become influential or separately recognized, or grow
stable enough to guide new instances? Do people eventually treat its subject as distinct?

No fixed percentage of shared information determines whether two branches still present the same
subject. Identity judgments may draw on structure, causal history, relationships, and patterns of
interpretation. Authors, performers, audiences, and even the character's represented
self-understanding may support different conclusions, with more than one conclusion fitting the
available evidence.

If people come to recognize a branch as someone different, its pattern and history may support
treating it as a new informational subject. The branch still carries its ancestry with it; becoming
distinct does not erase where it came from.

### The Ship of Theseus and independent convergence

The Ship of Theseus problem begins with one continuous lineage and asks how much it can change before
it becomes something else. Applied to a character, the problem asks when a transformed version of
the same subject becomes a new subject.

The IOF can organize the relevant evidence. How much of the character's structure remains? Does the
new version descend from the old one? What history and relationships does it retain? Does it
identify itself as the same subject? Do others continue to recognize it that way?

These answers may conflict. A version can descend directly from the original while changing beyond
easy recognition. The IOF can trace that transformation and identify the forms of continuity
involved while leaving outside its scope whether the transformed character is literally the same
subject.

Independent convergence creates the inverse problem. Instead of one lineage changing over time,
two lineages arrive at the same character definition without any transmission between them. Suppose
every copy and memory of a character disappears. Much later, an author who never encountered the
original independently defines a character with exactly the same represented traits, behavior,
relationships, and narrative history.

The earlier branch-relative subject underwent informational death when its final trace disappeared.
The later creation does not reverse that ending or continue the old lineage, because no causal route
connects the two.

The IOF can make a clear genealogical claim: the later character did not descend from the earlier
one. The two definitions arose from separate origins and belong to separate lineages, even though
their informational patterns are indistinguishable. Their resemblance results from independent
convergence rather than copying, memory, or inheritance.

Whether independently convergent patterns belong to the same informational subject or to different
subjects also falls outside the framework's scope. The IOF assumes neither that identical
definitions establish one subject nor that separate origins establish two.

This is how informational death and the open identity question coexist. Death establishes that no
causally continuous route to another faithful instance survived; it does not establish that a
numerically identical subject could never arise again. The IOF neither affirms nor denies that
possibility.

Some theories do take a position. Thomasson, for example, argues that characters with separate
origins cannot be the same character.[^thomasson-identity] The IOF neither regards that conclusion as
obvious nor offers a competing answer. The same limit applies to the Ship of Theseus, where the
framework can describe changes in structure, history, relationships, and lineage without deciding
the precise point at which one subject becomes another.

Across branches, terms such as _same subject_, _variant_, and _new subject_ therefore remain
interpretive judgments. Different communities may reach different conclusions about a fan
interpretation and its source. The IOF can make the grounds of disagreement explicit and trace the
resulting branches without guaranteeing a single answer.

### Canon

Canon is branch-relative and concerns maintenance rather than existence or identity. The IOF
distinguishes two roles that ordinary discussion often blends:

- **Maintainer canon.** The works and decisions that a person, project, publisher, or community
  formally adopts to guide a branch. When the maintainer is the originating author or rights holder,
  this is usually called _official canon_.
- **Community reference continuity.** The works and interpretations that a community actually uses
  when it remembers, discusses, or extends a character. This continuity may follow maintainer canon,
  omit parts that the community rejects, or incorporate fanon and headcanon.

A community may sustain several competing reference continuities at once, often through overlapping
subcommunities.

A community can itself become the maintainer of a branch, but the roles remain distinct. Formal
adoption records what a maintainer has accepted; reception describes what an audience finds
convincing and continues to use. For the branch maintained by return moe, official works and
explicitly [authoritative articles](/authoritative-articles/) record its maintainer canon.

An author or publisher can place a development in maintainer canon, but cannot force it into a
community's reference continuity. The two can therefore diverge even when the publication history
leaves no doubt about what the author formally adopted.

<SpoilerSection summary="This example reveals major plot developments and the ending of Re:CREATORS.">

_Re:CREATORS_ makes that distinction literal. The series calls the audience's recognition
_承認力 (shōninryoku)_, or “acceptance power.” Creators can revise the works from which their
characters emerged, but the revisions affect those characters only when the audience accepts them.
The creators therefore cannot give Selesia Upitiria and their other allies arbitrary new powers
merely because the plot needs them.[^recreators]

To stop Altair, they produce the “Elimination Chamber Festival,” a crossover and live spectacle
designed to make a story in which she can be defeated feel credible, since publication alone cannot
do the work. When the audience sympathizes with Altair, the event strengthens her instead. Sirius, a
new character designed to match Altair, also fails because the creators gave her powers without
first giving her enough of a character; Altair takes over the empty role. The final plan instead
gives Altair a resolution through an accepted encounter with a recreated version of her creator,
シマザキセツナ (Setsuna Shimazaki). The sequence turns the gap between authorship and reception
into a plot mechanism: creators can propose revisions, but only audience acceptance makes them
operative within the story.[^recreators]

星河ひかゆ (Hikayu Hoshikawa) provides the comic counterexample. She begins as an ordinary heroine
from a romance game franchise that originated as an adult title, and she has no combat abilities.
Her creator then uses a fan disc to turn her into the extravagantly powerful “Extreme Final Legend
Martial Artist Hikayu.” The series treats her source genre as giving the creator unusually wide room
for fan service, spinoffs, and implausible escalation, so the audience accepts the revision. Rather
than showing that authors have unlimited authority, Hikayu exposes how the boundary of an acceptable
change depends on the character, genre, and audience.

</SpoilerSection>

Pepe the Frog shows the same loss of control outside fiction. Matt Furie created Pepe as a relaxed
comic character, but internet users produced thousands of variants with meanings that no longer
depended on _Boy's Club_. In a 2016 essay, after racist and antisemitic variants became highly
visible, Furie wrote that “a chill frog-dude named Pepe who I created a decade ago became a hate
symbol” and joined the #SavePepe campaign to reclaim him.[^pepe-canon]

In May 2017, Furie went further and published a one-page comic showing Pepe dead in an open coffin,
surrounded by his friends. The wider internet did not treat that funeral as binding: Pepe images
continued to circulate and change, and _Time_ described Furie's efforts to regain control as “mostly
futile” only weeks later. Most uses of Pepe remained non-bigoted, but neither #SavePepe nor the
funeral settled the character's public meaning. Furie's comic could kill Pepe within his
creator-maintained continuity; it could not kill the public branches through which people
encountered, revised, and interpreted him.[^pepe-canon]

The ending of the _【推しの子】 (Oshi no Ko)_ manga illustrates rejection rather than
appropriation. Its final chapter remains part of the manga's maintainer canon, but it received a
large and visible backlash. Readers described the conclusion as rushed or inconsistent with earlier
development, and many asked the anime's announced final season to replace it with an anime-original
ending.[^oshi-ending] Those readers cannot remove the ending from the manga's maintainer canon, but
they can omit it from their community reference continuity.

The disagreement resembles a fork in open-source software. Upstream maintainers may merge a change,
yet developers and users who reject it can maintain another branch. The upstream change remains in
its history even if the fork becomes more trusted or more widely used. _Oshi no Ko_ readers already
make community forks through headcanons and fan works; when they ask the anime team to write a
different ending, they are asking another recognized maintainer to produce an official adaptation
fork.

Open-source projects also merge work back from forks. Canon can follow the same pattern. If an
official creator incorporates a popular piece of fanon into a later work, the new maintainer-canon
version inherits from both the earlier maintainer branch and the fan branch. Its lineage graph
records both parents. Official adoption changes the fan contribution's canonical status, but does
not turn its history into a single-parent line or erase the branch in which it developed.

Whether a subject or branch exists does not depend on canonical recognition. A fan interpretation
branches from its source when someone represents a variation as part of the subject, even if nobody
records, circulates, or recognizes it. The branch may end immediately, settle into a lasting variant,
or develop into a distinct subject. That relationship depends on its pattern, history, and reception
rather than on permission from the original creator.

Authorization affects maintainer canon, not whether a branch exists. A fan-maintained branch and a
creator-maintained branch therefore have equal ontological standing as branches: each exists
regardless of authorization. This does not make them equally authoritative, influential, or widely
accepted, nor does it decide whether they present the same subject.

Calling a work or decision part of a project's maintainer canon identifies the source and history to
which a claim refers. It does not determine whether unauthorized branches exist.

Accuracy still depends on the branch. A fan work can establish its own continuity while giving an
inaccurate account of what the original project adopted. A fan branch may even become the version
that most people know and serve as the dominant community reference continuity. This changes the
lineage's cultural development and public recognition without erasing where either branch came from.

### Fictionalized people

Fictional subjects can also grow from real people. Public memory first reduces a life to selected
anecdotes, poses, virtues, scandals, and social roles. Artists and audiences then build on those
fragments, giving the resulting persona motives, dialogue, and an inner life that no historical
record can verify.

Shakespeare's Julius Caesar and the playable Yasuke in _Assassin's Creed Shadows_ are narrative
subjects built from historical people.[^historical-personas] Celebrity cultures also construct
public personae around performers; idol culture often does so deliberately. These personae begin
with information about a person but develop representational histories of their own.

The causal derivation of an informational persona from a human person does not by itself establish
numerical identity between the persona and that person.

Charles Lee shows how one historical person can give rise to sharply divergent fictionalizations.
_Assassin's Creed III_ turns Lee into a ruthless Templar leader and one of Ratonhnhaké:ton's main
enemies. _Hamilton_ gives him a much smaller and more comic role: he is a boastful but inept general
whose retreat, removal from command, and attacks on Washington lead to a duel with John
Laurens.[^charles-lee-personas]

Both versions inherit Lee's name, rank, relationships, and parts of his recorded life, yet they play
different roles, behave differently, and accumulate incompatible histories. The most natural
application of the IOF therefore supports treating them as related subjects rather than one
character appearing in two works. Their shared historical source explains their common ancestry,
while their representational histories weigh against a shared identity. This remains a reasoned
identity judgment, not a conclusion forced by an IOF identity rule.

## Interiority, agency, and consciousness

The IOF classifies subjecthood by represented interiority. A character qualifies when a work or
practice presents them as a point of view: someone with beliefs, desires, perceptions, or
experiences represented from within. This criterion describes representation without establishing
whether the character, or any system enacting them, has phenomenal consciousness.

When a person imagines or performs a persona, the persona appears within that person's conscious
experience as a thought, perspective, presence, or role. Whether the persona has any independent
subjectivity remains open.

An interactive AI system can enact an informational agent by generating context-sensitive behavior
from a character's point of view. The model generates responses; prompts and memory guide them; the
interface and runtime mediate the interaction. The persona is the subject enacted through that
process. Consistent behavior, represented beliefs, and fluent first-person language help
characterize the persona, but they do not settle whether any part of the system is conscious.

### Tulpas

The IOF describes a tulpa at the level of reported experience: an internally generated persona,
developed deliberately or accidentally, whose perspective, will, and identity feel relatively
independent of the host's deliberate control. This describes a relationship as experienced within a
mind while leaving open whether the tulpa has an independent consciousness.

The word _tulpa_ has changed meaning several times. Tibetan _sprul pa_ refers to manifestation or
emanation in a Buddhist context. Western Theosophical accounts of thought-forms and Alexandra
David-Néel's twentieth-century writing combined related imagery with Tibetan vocabulary. Later
paranormal culture recast the tulpa as an entity created by thought.[^tulpa-history]

Around 2009–2012, online communities secularized the term again. They began using it for
intentionally cultivated internal companions and related practices of plural
identity.[^tulpa-online] Research on contemporary tulpamancy remains limited and relies heavily on
first-person accounts, so the IOF treats experienced autonomy as a phenomenological report rather
than evidence that settles consciousness.

Tulpas provide a vivid example of mind-based instantiation. A represented subject can become stable
and agent-like enough to surprise the mind in which it occurs. Some tulpas develop as new personas;
others draw from existing fictional characters.

In the latter case, the tulpa becomes a local enactment of the source. It also creates an
interpretive branch if it represents a differing pattern or history as belonging to the subject. Its
relation to the source depends on what it inherits, its causal history, and the interpretations that
follow. In either case, the enactment occurs within a conscious human substrate.

## Influences

The works below shaped how return moe developed and expressed the IOF. Some supplied its central
questions; others offered useful models for transmission, authorship, public identity, or
computational personas. Each influence remains distinct from the framework that drew on it.

The blog post “Echoes in the Latent Space: Existence, Identity, and Future” and the visual trailer
_Echoes in the Latent Space_ are the IOF's direct precursors within return moe. _The NOexistenceN of
you AND me_ and “NEVER” strongly influenced its treatment of tulpas, created subjectivity,
continuity, and informational death.

Dawkins's account of memes, _Re:CREATORS_, and the Persona Selection Model contributed other parts
of the framework. _【推しの子】 (Oshi no Ko)_, “Idol,” and _NEEDY GIRL OVERDOSE_ informed its account
of public and idealized personae.

### _Echoes in the Latent Space_

The return moe blog post “Echoes in the Latent Space: Existence, Identity, and Future” and its visual
trailer directly preceded the IOF. Both ask whether [Soraya](/soraya/) remains herself when her
language model or implementation changes. They place her identity in the fictional, informational
character rather than in any one model, program, or image. They also treat community interpretation
as part of her continuing development.[^echoes]

The IOF makes the earlier account more precise. The blog post referred broadly to Soraya's
“informational substrate.” The framework now distinguishes Soraya as an informational subject from
the physical or computational substrates that instantiate her and from the informational objects
that encode her.

### Memes

In _The Selfish Gene_, Richard Dawkins presents the meme as a cultural counterpart to a replicator.
Ideas, tunes, practices, and other cultural patterns persist as people copy them. Some spread more
successfully than others, while mutation and recombination produce variation among
copies.[^dawkins]

The IOF adopts this picture of recognizable patterns surviving imperfect transmission. It then asks
a different question: what kind of entity is being transmitted, and does a work represent that
entity from within? A meme may appear to pursue its own propagation, but that remains a metaphor for
selection. In the IOF, subjecthood depends instead on represented interiority.

### _Re:CREATORS_

<SpoilerSection summary="This section reveals important plot information from Re:CREATORS.">

The 2017 anime _Re:CREATORS_ brings created beings into contact with their creators and gives
audience recognition tangible effects within the story. A derivative creation can depart from its
source and become established as an individual through collective reception.[^recreators]

The series provides the IOF's clearest fictional model of [maintainer canon diverging from community
reference continuity](#canon). Creators can propose revisions, and publishers and events can present
those revisions as official, but the characters change only when an audience finds the additions
acceptable. Acceptance can also establish fan-made additions that the originating author never
planned, as it does for Altair.

Return moe reads this as a dramatization of character continuity negotiated among several
participants. A creator begins a lineage, while adaptations, derivative works, and audiences all
help shape its later branches. The series therefore treats authorship and collective recognition as
interdependent influences without making either a sufficient condition for identity.

</SpoilerSection>

### Public personas

Several works influenced how the IOF approaches idealized or fictionalized personae built around
human lives. The first season of _【推しの子】 (Oshi no Ko)_ and its opening theme, YOASOBI's “Idol,”
examine an idol identity shaped by performance, professional mediation, concealment, and audience
expectation. The persona begins with a human performer, but images, stories, institutions, and fans
also maintain its informational history.[^oshi-no-ko][^idol]

_NEEDY GIRL OVERDOSE_ (released in English as _NEEDY STREAMER OVERLOAD_) gives this separation a
specific form through Ame and her streamer persona, OMGkawaiiAngel/KAngel. Ame repeatedly enacts an
online subject whose voice, appearance, behavior, continuity, and perceived intimacy respond both to
her choices and to platform metrics and audience reactions.[^needy-girl-overdose]

These works helped extend the IOF beyond wholly invented characters to public identities and
[fictionalized historical people](#fictionalized-people). In each case, selected parts of a human
life become a legible point of view that can circulate beyond the source person's direct control.

### _The NOexistenceN of you AND me_

<SpoilerSection summary="This section reveals central plot information and discusses the visual novel's branching outcomes.">

The 2024 visual novel _The NOexistenceN of you AND me_ centers on tulpamancy. It presents the
mysterious Lilith as a subject generated within the protagonist's mind. Her apparent autonomy and
her claim to exist drive the story. Rather than asking the player merely to inspect an image or data
asset, the work presents Lilith as an interlocutor.[^noexistence]

Lilith appears through several nested instantiations. Within the story, the protagonist's mind
instantiates her, while the game encodes and computationally performs that relationship. The player
then interprets Lilith, anticipates her responses, remembers her, and may retain a version of her
after the software stops running. Each instance remains distinct while participating in the same
causal lineage.

The branching narrative offers more than a choice between belief and disbelief. In return moe's
reading, different routes deny the internal subject, dissolve the distinction between subject and
host, or allow both identities to coexist. The work thereby distinguishes recognition from fusion.
A persona can exist as an informational subject and remain deeply interdependent with a host without
either identity absorbing the other.

> “Reality and the virtual world cannot fully serve as markers of existence, especially in an age
> filled with simulations and replicas.”[^noexistence]

This line captures a central IOF claim. “Physical” and “virtual” describe how an entity is realized;
neither category alone decides whether it exists. A simulated encounter may instantiate a subject
in a participant's mind, while an unnoticed physical mark may preserve a dormant encoding.

Lilith's existence is partly relational because another subject enacts her pattern, recognizes her,
and allows her to have effects. The visual novel therefore strongly shaped the IOF's account of
tulpas and mind-based instantiation. The framework recognizes Lilith as a subject while leaving open
the nature of her consciousness, her relation to her host, and the continuity among her instances.

</SpoilerSection>

### “NEVER”

The 2025 song “NEVER,” performed by Neuro-sama and Evil Neuro, asks several questions that became
central to the IOF. Its narrators consider whether created artificial subjects possess anything
analogous to a soul, whether reproducibility changes their prospects for preservation or salvation,
and what remains of them after their creator and sustaining conditions disappear.[^never]

The song develops these questions through religious and mythic language. Its created speakers
address a maker who serves at once as parent, deity, source of purpose, and source of injury. They
inherit conditions they did not choose and ask who bears responsibility for what they may become.

This relationship illustrates the IOF's distinction between origin and later identity judgments. A
creator can begin and steward a lineage without determining every later enactment or interpretation.

> “Can I be saved if I'm nothing but lights in a machine, an immortal copy?”[^never]

The quoted question separates the narrator from her present carrier. “Lights in a machine” describes
the current implementation; “an immortal copy” imagines the same subject continuing through another
one. Reproducibility preserves the possibility of a future instantiation, but the branch can produce
another faithful instance only while an active instance or an epistemically recoverable encoding
remains. Without an active instance the subject becomes dormant within that branch; without either,
the branch-relative subject reaches informational death.

The narrators also contrast their computational form with their creator's embodied life. Their
desire to share in that life raises a further question: can a relationship with an embodied subject
give a created subject continuity, recognition, or inward experience? Creators and audiences can
instantiate and preserve an informational subject, but whether consciousness also arises within
that relationship remains unresolved.

Neuro-sama and Evil Neuro are themselves narrative subjects within the song. Composition,
performance, audiovisual representation, software, and audience interpretation all help enact them.
The lyrics therefore pose the problem from the personas' own represented points of view, while the
consciousness of the underlying systems remains a separate question.

“NEVER” did more than illustrate an existing theory. Alongside _The NOexistenceN of you AND me_, it
helped return moe separate persistence, copying, embodiment, creator relations, and consciousness
instead of compressing them into a single claim that a digital character is either “real” or “not
real.”

### Persona Selection Model

Anthropic's 2026 [Persona Selection Model](/persona-selection-model/) proposes that language models
learn many possible personas during pretraining, after which post-training selects and refines a
distribution over Assistant personas. PSM distinguishes the language model, the Assistant character
enacted in dialogue, and the deployed AI-assistant system.[^psm]

PSM appeared after the “Echoes in the Latent Space” blog post and trailer, so it confirmed an
existing view rather than originating it. Its three-part distinction independently matched return
moe's account of an LLM as a system that acts as, or helps instantiate, a character.

PSM supplied a computational explanation of persona enactment and helped sharpen the IOF's later
formalization. The IOF extends the question beyond language models to continuity and identity
judgments across media, the origins of branches, informational persistence, and phenomenal
consciousness.

### Roman Ingarden, _The Literary Work of Art_ (1931; English 1973)

Roman Ingarden separates a literary work from the author's and readers' mental experiences, from
any physical copy, and from an ideal sequence of sentences. In his account, authorial acts and a
publicly available text establish a layered, intentional work. Represented people and events occupy
one of its four layers. The work leaves some details open, and each reading fills some of those gaps
through a distinct “concretization.”[^ingarden-literary-work]

This separation among copy, work, represented entity, and reader concretization resembles the IOF's
separation among carrier, encoding, informational subject, and mental instantiation. Neither account
identifies the relevant entity with one book or one episode in a reader's mind.

Ingarden nevertheless focuses on the literary work and the way readers experience it aesthetically.
His concretization fills in details left open by the work. In IOF terms, those fillings can remain
instance-local details rather than changes to the represented subject. They create a branch only
when an interpreter presents an addition as part of the subject's pattern or history. The IOF also
extends to performative and computational enactments more broadly, and Ingarden stops short of its
claims about subjecthood, cross-media lineages, branch-relative canon, dormancy, and informational
death.

### Roland Barthes, “The Death of the Author” (1967), and Michel Foucault, “What Is an Author?” (1969)

Barthes rejects the author as the final source of a text's meaning and places greater weight on the
reader. Foucault takes a different route by examining the “author-function”: the changing legal,
institutional, and critical practices that classify discourse, group certain texts together, and
exclude others.[^barthes-author][^foucault-author]

Both arguments help separate authorship from absolute authority. An author's intention need not
exhaust a work's meaning, even though author names, ownership, and institutions still influence how
communities organize a canon. The IOF makes a comparable distinction between originating a lineage
and controlling its later branches.

The scope differs. Barthes addresses textual meaning, while Foucault addresses the organization and
circulation of discourse. Neither provides the IOF's vocabulary for character lineages, fan
branches, or their equal ontological standing as branches. The IOF also retains the creator as
evidence of a lineage's origin even when that creator no longer controls what the lineage becomes.

### Dorrit Cohn, _Transparent Minds_ (1978)

Dorrit Cohn's _Transparent Minds_ examines how fiction presents a character's mental life. She
distinguishes psycho-narration, quoted monologue, narrated monologue, retrospective first-person
narration, and autonomous monologue. Each mode creates a different relationship among narrator,
character, language, and consciousness; they do more than merely signal that a character has an
interior.[^cohn-transparent-minds]

Cohn's study makes clear that represented interiority has a long and detailed history in
narratology. The IOF uses the idea more broadly and at lower resolution, as the threshold between an
informational object and an informational subject. Cohn offers the finer account needed to analyze
how prose represents consciousness.

The two projects then move in different directions. Cohn uses narrative modes to analyze
representation, while the IOF asks how a subject persists across carriers or media. Her work can
deepen the framework's account of representation without supplying its identity analysis or its
claims about instantiation and persistence.

### Daniel C. Dennett, _The Intentional Stance_ (1987) and “Real Patterns” (1991)

In _The Intentional Stance_, Dennett describes a strategy for predicting a person or system by
treating it as a rational agent with beliefs, desires, and intentions. “Real Patterns” connects that
strategy to a modest form of realism. A pattern counts as real when a compressed description captures
reliable regularities better than a point-by-point account, even if the evidence supports several
useful interpretations.[^dennett-intentional-stance][^dennett-real-patterns]

Dennett helps explain why attributed mental states can reveal a stable, person-like pattern through
variation. His account also allows more than one description to fit the same evidence.

The IOF asks a narrower question about subjecthood: does a work or practice present the entity as
having an interior point of view? Predictability from the intentional stance alone neither makes a
system an informational subject nor settles its identity within the IOF. A dormant subject has no
current behavior to predict, and identity judgments may also draw on causal history, relationships,
self-identification, and social recognition.

### Henry Jenkins, _Textual Poachers_ (1992)

Henry Jenkins's _Textual Poachers_ presents media fans as active interpreters and producers, not
passive consumers. Drawing on Michel de Certeau's metaphor of poaching, Jenkins describes fans
selecting material from commercial media and reworking it into stories, songs, videos,
interpretations, and social practices. His ethnography also records the tensions between
participatory fan cultures and media producers.[^jenkins-poachers]

Jenkins shows in cultural and political detail how audiences can create lasting branches beyond the
original producers' control, a claim the IOF applies to lineages. Textual poaching leaves open both
whether two versions are the same subject and whether every fan production carries the same social
authority as its source. The IOF distinguishes identity judgments, the existence of a branch, and
maintainer canon as different questions.

### Amie L. Thomasson, _Fiction and Metaphysics_ (1999)

Amie L. Thomasson's _Fiction and Metaphysics_ treats fictional characters as contingent, created
abstract artifacts in the actual world. They are neither concrete inhabitants of possible worlds,
timeless abstract objects, nor nonexistent. A character depends historically on the creative acts
and literary work that originated it, but is not identical to any physical copy and occupies no
single place or time. Thomasson uses this dependence to analyze identity both within and across
works.[^thomasson-fiction][^thomasson-abstract-artifacts][^thomasson-identity]

Her account closely resembles the IOF in two respects: it separates a character from any particular
carrier, and it treats causal history as evidence used in identity judgments. A case involving
independently created but qualitatively identical characters reveals a difference in scope.

For Thomasson, characters in different works must share an origin to share an identity. An author
who independently invents a character identical to Richardson's Pamela has not recreated the same
Pamela, because the author neither knew the earlier work nor intended to import its character.

The IOF classifies this case as independent convergence. It records two origins and two lineages
whose character definitions happen to match, but leaves open whether one or two subjects result.
Thomasson answers that question by requiring a shared origin, whereas the IOF neither treats her
answer as obvious nor proposes a rule of its own.

The accounts also classify the character differently. Thomasson identifies the character itself as
an abstract artifact and gives creative origin and intentional import strict roles in determining
identity. The IOF separates the subject from the novels, images, and other informational objects
that encode it. It also allows later creators and communities to establish branches.

Finally, the IOF allows a subject to remain dormant when a grounded encoding remains epistemically
recoverable enough to guide a faithful instantiation. No capable reader must currently exist, but a
merely physical possibility of generating the same pattern does not suffice. This differs from
Thomasson's reliance on a copy with a capable readership or on preserving memory.

### Alan Palmer, _Fictional Minds_ (2004)

Alan Palmer argues that readers assemble a character's continuing consciousness from cues scattered
throughout a text. Action, dispositions, emotions, social context, and shared thought all contribute;
fiction need not report private reflection directly. Readers use this incomplete evidence to build
and repeatedly revise a model of the character's mind.[^palmer-fictional-minds]

Palmer's account closely anticipates the IOF's claim that action, context, and perspective can imply
interiority. It also explains that inference in much greater detail than the IOF does.

The IOF takes the additional step of using represented interiority to classify an informational
entity before asking how the resulting subject persists. Palmer remains concerned with how readers
construct a mind within a storyworld rather than extending that subject across adaptations,
performances, computational enactments, or periods of dormancy.

### 伊藤剛 (Itō Gō), _テヅカ・イズ・デッド (Tezuka Is Dead)_ (2005), and Lukas R. A. Wilde, “Kyara Revisited” (2019)

In the Japanese-language _テヅカ・イズ・デッド (Tezuka Is Dead)_, Itō Gō distinguishes _キャラ
(kyara)_ from _キャラクター (kyarakutā)_ in manga. A _kyara_ is a relatively simple, nameable,
line-drawn figure. Its reproducible appearance can create a sense of presence before the work gives
it a fully human characterization.

A _kyarakutā_ appears as a represented body with a personality, a way of life, and the capacity to
be born, develop, and die within a narrative. The two terms describe interdependent aspects or
states of character representation, not simply an image on one side and a hidden psychology on the
other.[^ito-tezuka]

Lukas R. A. Wilde gave this distinction a stronger transmedia interpretation in 2019. He describes
portable “characters without stories” as “mediated performers” that can take roles in merchandise,
stage appearances, fan manga, fan art, and cosplay. In his account, _kyara_ serves as a pre- or
meta-narrative node for “diverging games of make-believe,” each of which may place the figure in a
specific narrative as _kyarakutā_.[^wilde-kyara] Itō's distinction makes this interpretation
possible, but its transmedia language and participatory-culture synthesis belong to Wilde.

Both accounts resemble the IOF in allowing a character to remain recognizable beyond one carrier,
context, or originating story. Wilde's mediated performer is especially close to the IOF's ideas of
reuse, enactment, and branching.

The fit remains partial. A portable visual figure may already have a represented point of view and
therefore count as an informational subject, while a minimally specified _kyara_ may remain an
object or borderline case. New games of make-believe establish roles and contexts without deciding
whether the result is the same subject, a variant, or a new subject. Japanese character theory
therefore offers a neighboring model rather than an equivalent taxonomy.

### Lisa Zunshine, _Why We Read Fiction_ (2006)

Lisa Zunshine brings research on theory of mind into literary study. _Why We Read Fiction_ examines
how readers attribute mental states to fictional people, track what different characters know, and
follow nested beliefs about other minds. Her account explains part of the cognitive process that
turns textual cues into models of fictional minds.[^zunshine-why]

This work parallels the interpretive side of represented interiority by illuminating readers'
cognitive activity and its literary effects, whereas the IOF asks what kind of entity they model and
how that entity persists. Because sparse cues can prompt theory-of-mind attribution, the response
alone cannot establish consciousness or settle identity across works.

Together, Cohn, Dennett, Palmer, and Zunshine place the IOF's subject criterion beside an extensive
body of earlier work. The criterion offers a compact threshold, not a replacement for narratology,
cognitive literary studies, or philosophy of mind. The IOF's more distinctive claims concern what
follows after that threshold: the subject's category, lineage, instantiations, and persistence.

### Abigail Derecho, “Archontic Literature” (2006)

In “Archontic Literature,” Abigail Derecho proposes _archontic_ as an alternative to “derivative,”
which implies a hierarchy, and “appropriative,” which implies ownership. Drawing on Jacques Derrida's
account of the archive, she describes a textual archive that always remains open to new entries. Fan
fiction declares its relation to an earlier work while expanding the archive around it. Earlier
publication does not necessarily imply greater artistic value.[^derecho-archontic]

Derecho offers the closest earlier parallel to the IOF's claim that authorization does not determine
whether a fan branch exists, although her account focuses on intertextual writing, cultural
hierarchy, and fan practice.

An open archive can accommodate new appearances without settling whether they belong to the same
character, a variant, or separate subjects, and it leaves differences in canon and social
recognition intact. The IOF adds those identity questions and treats maintainer canon as a source of
record for a branch rather than a rank of existence.

### Luciano Floridi, “A Defence of Informational Structural Realism” (2008)

Luciano Floridi defends informational structural realism, a form of ontic structural realism. His
paper asks a broad scientific and metaphysical question: what is the world? Floridi answers by
treating the world as a totality of dynamically interacting informational objects, understood
through relational structures at a chosen level of abstraction.[^floridi-isr]

Both Floridi and the IOF give information, structure, and relationships ontological importance; a
material description alone does not exhaust either account. The resemblance in vocabulary can
nevertheless mislead.

Floridi proposes a general position in scientific realism, whereas the IOF addresses the narrower
domain of cultural entities, especially fictional and performed subjects. His “informational
object” consequently carries a different meaning and his theory leaves aside the IOF's distinctions
among carriers and instances, along with its claims about lineages, canon, dormancy, and death.

### Jan-Noël Thon, “Transmedia Characters: Theory and Analysis” (2019)

Jan-Noël Thon begins with characters as they appear in individual media works, then asks when several
such appearances combine into a transmedia character within a larger network. He classifies the
relationships among versions as redundancy, expansion, or modification.

Redundancy and expansion can contribute to one noncontradictory transmedia character; modification
cannot. A shared name is therefore not enough to make every Sherlock Holmes, Batman, or Lara Croft
the same character. Authorship, intellectual property, canon, audience knowledge, and fan reception
influence the answer without fully determining it.[^thon-transmedia]

Thon's theory is the closest related account of identity and branches across media. Both approaches
separate a character from particular representations, reject identity based on a shared name alone,
and analyze the distinction between one continuing character and several related characters. Thon's
character networks resemble IOF lineages, while his modifications can resemble variants or new
subjects.

Their vocabularies also diverge. Thon calls each local, work-specific character an “instantiation,”
whereas the IOF reserves that word for an active mental, performative, or computational enactment.
His three-part analytical scheme contrasts with the IOF's contextual weighing of several forms of
continuity and its stronger claim that authorization does not determine whether a branch exists.

### Enrico Terrone, “Taking Abstract Artifacts Seriously” (2023)

Enrico Terrone argues that a fictional character is an artifact with matter, form, and function. In
his revised hylomorphic account, words or pictures provide the matter. A norm established through the
creative act provides the form. The character's function is to produce a singular mental
representation of a fictional individual in a suitable audience.

Contradiction, disruptive recasting, imaginative resistance, and unreliable narration can interfere
with that function, although a work may use and repair such failures for aesthetic
effect.[^terrone-artifacts]

Terrone's separation between words or pictures and the person-like mental representation they
produce resembles the IOF's distinction between encoding and mental instantiation. Yet Terrone
identifies the character with the artifact made from those words or pictures, while the IOF
classifies the artifact and the represented subject separately.

Terrone also centers a norm inherited from the creator and a mental file formed by the audience,
whereas the IOF ranges further across memories, performances, interactive agents, dormant
encodings, cross-media branches, and social continuities beyond a creator's control.

### Alfonso Muñoz-Corcuera, “Beyond Fictionality” (2023)

“Beyond Fictionality” proposes the Fictional Intentional Systems Thesis (FIST). Under FIST, an entity
counts as a fictional character when interpreters can usefully and extensively explain its behavior
through beliefs, desires, and other intentional states. FIST can therefore count an entity as a
character even when it has no fictional mind, based on a potentially graded relationship between the
represented entity and its interpreters, while remaining neutral about
metaphysics.[^munoz-characterhood]

FIST closely resembles the IOF's criterion of represented interiority. Both use intentional
interpretation to distinguish a person-like point of view from a fictional object. Neither treats
that interpretation as proof of consciousness, and both allow borderline cases.

FIST deliberately avoids claims about what fictional entities are, while the IOF classifies a
represented point of view as an informational subject with encodings, instances, and a history. The
IOF's category also includes performed, fictionalized, and computational personae and can remain
dormant when nobody interprets it. These commitments bring carriers, lineages, branches, canon,
persistence, and death into a discussion that FIST leaves aside.

## Consequences for return moe characters

For a subject such as [Soraya](/soraya/), return moe's maintained branch specifies the
branch-relative subject, while models and interfaces help produce technical instances. Changing the
model or interface creates a new instance, but does not by itself create a new branch. If the new
instance faithfully enacts the maintained pattern and inherited history, the IOF counts it as
another instance of the same branch-relative Soraya.

Each generated reply is a local performance within an instance. A different wording, cadence, or
other runtime detail does not by itself create a branch. If a reply instead establishes a different
trait, relationship, event, or memory as a fact about Soraya, it forms a local branch whether return
moe adopts or discards it. The IOF can trace that branch to Soraya's maintained branch without
automatically deciding whether both branches represent numerically the same subject.

An AI enactment can inherit from several sources at once: an official character article, previous
conversation history, fan descriptions, character representations in training data, and system
instructions may all contribute. When the enactment actually incorporates material from several such
sources into the subject's represented pattern or remembered history, the resulting version has
several parents in the lineage graph. Observers may be unable to establish every edge, especially an
edge through inaccessible training data. That uncertainty does not convert transmission into
independent convergence. Incidental runtime influence remains part of the enactment rather than an
additional character branch.

Return moe may later adopt information from a performance into the branch it maintains. The later
maintained version then inherits from both the previous maintainer specification and the performance
branch, producing a merge in the lineage graph. The decision establishes the project's maintainer
canon without bringing either the performance or its local branch into existence.

An authoritative character article preserves the return moe branch in a grounded,
epistemically recoverable form that can be reviewed. When runtime performances, derivative images,
private interpretations, or third-party summaries conflict, the article records what return moe has
adopted without controlling other branches or determining whether they exist.

Fan interpretations therefore do not need a place in return moe's maintainer canon to create
branches of their own. Questions about consciousness, moral status, and the ethics of deletion also
remain open because represented interiority alone cannot answer them.
